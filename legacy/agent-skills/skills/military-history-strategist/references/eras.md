# Warfare by Era — Quick Reference

Notes on what actually changes across eras, so a fictional scenario or a
historical sanity-check is grounded in the right mechanics. Read only the
section relevant to the era in question.

## Ancient (roughly to ~500 CE)

- **Command reach**: a general could directly influence only what he could
  see or reach by messenger on foot/horse within the battle itself. Battles
  were largely decided before they started, by formation and position.
- **Dominant arms**: massed infantry (phalanx, legion, etc.), with cavalry
  and skirmishers on the flanks. Missile weapons (bow, sling, javelin)
  softened before the melee decided it.
- **Logistics**: armies moved along rivers/coasts or lived off the land;
  campaign range was limited by how far you could forage or cart supplies
  before starving. Sieges were slow (starvation, not breach) unless the
  attacker had serious siege engineering.
- **Battle duration**: a field battle was typically hours, not days — once a
  line broke, it broke completely (routs, not orderly withdrawal).
- **What decides it**: discipline and cohesion of formation under pressure,
  terrain, and whether one side could turn a flank.

## Medieval (roughly 500–1400 CE)

- **Command reach**: similar constraints to ancient warfare, but feudal
  levies added a political dimension — a lord's contingent might leave if he
  judged the cause lost or his obligation fulfilled.
- **Dominant arms**: heavy cavalry (knights) as the shock arm in much of
  Europe, supported by infantry and increasingly effective missile troops
  (longbow, crossbow) that could stop a cavalry charge given the right
  terrain and formation.
- **Logistics**: castles and fortified towns dominate the operational
  picture — campaigns were often about controlling strongpoints, not seeking
  open battle, because sieges (now with real engineering: trebuchets,
  sapping, later early gunpowder) were where campaigns were actually won.
- **What decides it**: often politics and attrition (who can keep an army in
  the field / a siege going) as much as any single battle.

## Gunpowder / Early Modern (roughly 1400–1800)

- **Command reach**: still limited to line-of-sight and mounted couriers, but
  standing armies and drill allowed larger, more coordinated formations.
- **Dominant arms**: transition from pike-and-shot to massed musket lines
  with bayonets, plus increasingly decisive field artillery. Cavalry shifts
  from shock-breaker to flank/pursuit/screening role as infantry firepower
  rises.
- **Logistics**: the birth of the supply train and magazine system —
  campaigns are now explicitly bounded by how far an army can operate from
  its depots. "An army marches on its stomach" is a logistics-first era.
- **Battle duration**: still usually a single day, but casualties from
  massed volley fire are far higher than earlier eras.
- **What decides it**: firepower discipline (standing in line under fire),
  artillery concentration, and logistics reach.

## Industrial (roughly 1800–1914)

- **Command reach**: telegraph and rail change everything — a commander can
  now direct forces beyond sight and move them faster than they can march.
  This is also the first era where mobilization *schedules* (rail timetables)
  become a strategic factor in their own right.
- **Dominant arms**: rifled muskets/breechloaders, then repeating rifles and
  early machine guns, make the open-field massed charge suicidal by the
  century's end. Artillery becomes vastly more lethal and long-ranged.
- **Logistics**: railroads extend campaign range enormously but create a
  dependency on the rail line itself as a target.
- **What decides it**: firepower now punishes concentration — the era ends
  with armies discovering (often at horrific cost) that they need to dig in
  rather than advance in the open.

## Modern (1914–present)

- **Command reach**: radio, then satellite/digital communications give
  near-real-time command over forces far beyond sight — command friction
  shifts from "can I reach them" to "can I process information fast enough."
- **Dominant arms**: combined arms is the baseline assumption — infantry,
  armor, artillery, and air support are expected to coordinate, not act
  alone. Airpower and (later) precision munitions add a vertical dimension
  ground-only eras didn't have.
- **Logistics**: fuel and ammunition consumption rates are enormous compared
  to any earlier era; a modern army's campaign range is bounded by fuel and
  ammunition resupply, not food.
- **What decides it**: combined-arms coordination, intelligence/information
  advantage, and logistics throughput (especially fuel) more than any single
  arm's quality.
